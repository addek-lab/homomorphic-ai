Four whitepapers, four levels of evidence

I collected four whitepapers on homomorphic encryption and privacy-preserving AI and read them in one sitting. They all say roughly the same thing in their conclusions: HE is powerful, slow, and getting better. Underneath that agreement, they are very different kinds of document. Sorting them by how much evidence they give turned out to be the most useful thing I did.

The four, from strongest to weakest evidence

  1. A research paper (Zama, Chillotti–Joye–Paillier). Methods, parameter tables, 1,000-run benchmarks, accuracy numbers, and a stated baseline. I could check what was measured and on what hardware.
  2. An industry survey (Kruman). A genuinely useful map of FHE, federated learning, differential privacy and synthetic data. But its references are mostly blogs, FAQ pages and press releases, so a lot of the claims are second-hand.
  3. A services whitepaper (Tymon Global). Clear on the pipeline pattern and on adoption steps. No references, and it exists to sell consulting.
  4. An overview (Onegen.ai). A tidy introduction with no numbers and no citations. The LLM sections describe aspirations, not results.

Where they contradict each other

The most instructive part was the disagreements.

  • Speed. One paper says bootstrapping can make encrypted ML take "days instead of hours". The research paper measured a 100-layer network at roughly 11–24 seconds on a 96-vCPU server (80-bit security). Both can be "true" for different schemes, years and workloads. But only one of them says which.
  • What to adopt first. Kruman's survey ranks federated learning and differential privacy as the most deployment-ready, and keeps HE for high-security cases. Tymon pitches HE as the strategic baseline for cloud AI. The honest reading: it depends on the threat model.
  • Forecasts. Tymon projects 10–100× speedups in one place and 5–10× in another. Neither has a basis. I treat these as opinions.

My rules for reading vendor papers

  1. Numbers need a setup. A figure without scheme, parameters, hardware and baseline is a talking point, not data.
  2. Separate measured, cited and asserted. I mark each claim with one of the three.
  3. Check scope before comparing. "Privacy tech market" and "HE market" are different markets, so their growth rates can't be compared.
  4. Product names are claims. If a paper says a cloud provider ships a feature, I verify it in that provider's docs.
The result of this exercise is on the Knowledge Base page: the concepts, an explorer for the one real benchmark, and a table of claims with my assessment.
