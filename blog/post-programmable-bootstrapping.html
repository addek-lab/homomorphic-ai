Programmable bootstrapping, explained

These are my notes on Programmable Bootstrapping Enables Efficient Homomorphic Inference of Deep Neural Networks by Chillotti, Joye and Paillier (Zama). It is the most concrete of the four whitepapers I read, because it shows what actually runs.

The problem it attacks

Earlier encrypted neural networks, like CryptoNets, worked around FHE's limits by changing the network: the ReLU activation was replaced with squaring, and depth was kept shallow because noise grows with every multiplication. That means retraining a different model, which is expensive and sometimes impossible, for example when you can't access the training data or a regulator must re-approve any model change.

The idea in three steps

  1. Noise resets need bootstrapping. Decrypting a ciphertext "inside the encryption" produces a fresh, low-noise one. That's Gentry's trick.
  2. Bootstrapping is a rotation. In TFHE, the decryption turns into rotating a polynomial by an encrypted amount (a blind rotation). The polynomial is the "test polynomial".
  3. Put a function in the polynomial. If that polynomial holds a lookup table instead of the identity, the same rotation returns f(x) with fresh noise. One operation resets the noise and evaluates the function. That is the programmable part.
noisy Enc(x)  --blind rotate-->  clean Enc(f(x))
                (f stored as a lookup table)

What it means for a network

Linear layers (dense, convolution, addition, average pooling) only multiply by clear weights and add, which are cheap leveled operations. Non-linear layers use PBS: ReLU is one lookup, and max-pooling over k values costs k−1 lookups using max(x,y) = y + ReLU(x−y). Because every PBS leaves the noise at a fixed level, depth stops being a limit.

What the benchmarks show

The authors built 20-, 50- and 100-layer networks on MNIST and compared them with plaintext inference (ONNX Runtime). A few points I noted:

  • At 80-bit security the 20-layer net took 2.85 s on a 96-vCPU server and 12.49 s on a laptop, at 97.2% accuracy vs 97.5% in the clear.
  • At 128-bit security (parameter set III) the 100-layer net took 81 s on the server, but accuracy fell to 83.0%.
  • The plaintext run takes well under a millisecond, so the slowdown is in the range of tens of thousands to over a million times.

You can try the numbers yourself in the cost explorer.

What I'm still unsure about

  • Why accuracy falls for the deepest network even at higher security settings. The paper discusses noise and "drift" from rescaling, but I'd want to reproduce it before drawing a conclusion.
  • How this scales past MNIST. The paper is a feasibility result on a small dataset in 2020, and not a prediction for large models.
  • Independence. The authors build the library they benchmark. That doesn't make the results wrong, but it makes independent replication more valuable.
