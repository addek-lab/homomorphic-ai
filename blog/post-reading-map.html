A reading map: from Gentry (2009) to CKKS and TFHE

When I started on Homomorphic AI, the hardest part wasn't the math. It was not knowing which papers to read in which order. FHE literature is a family tree, and reading it out of order feels like walking into the middle of an argument. This is the order I settled on.

1. Start with the idea, not the construction

Craig Gentry's 2009 thesis and STOC paper, Fully Homomorphic Encryption Using Ideal Lattices, is the first plausible construction of an encryption scheme that supports both addition and multiplication on ciphertexts. I don't try to follow every proof. I read it to understand two ideas that everything afterwards builds on:

  • Noise growth. Every ciphertext carries a small amount of noise. Operations make it grow, and multiplication makes it grow fast. Decryption fails if it gets too large.
  • Bootstrapping. Homomorphically evaluating the decryption circuit to "refresh" a ciphertext and reset its noise.

2. The second generation: BGV and BFV

The 2012 papers by Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan (BGV) and Brakerski / Fan–Vercauteren (BFV) rebuilt FHE on the Ring-LWE problem. They are far more practical and give exact arithmetic on integers. This is where I started to understand parameters: polynomial degree, ciphertext modulus and plaintext modulus, and how they trade security against depth and speed.

3. CKKS: why it matters for machine learning

Cheon, Kim, Kim and Song (2017) introduced a scheme for approximate arithmetic on real or complex numbers. For AI this is the natural fit. Neural networks are floating-point and already tolerate small errors, so a bit of controlled imprecision is acceptable. I treat CKKS as the default candidate for encrypted inference on linear-heavy models.

4. TFHE: fast bootstrapping and non-linearity

Chillotti, Gama, Georgieva and Izabachène (2016) showed bootstrapping that is fast enough to be applied after every gate. Later work on programmable bootstrapping lets you evaluate a lookup table, which is a clean way to handle non-linear functions like ReLU. This is the foundation of tools such as Zama's Concrete ML.

5. The bridge to ML

Finally, I read the applied papers. CryptoNets (Gilad-Bachrach et al., 2016) was an early demonstration of running a neural network on encrypted data, using a polynomial activation in place of ReLU. Reading it after the theory makes its design choices obvious. You can see the model being shaped around what the encryption allows.

How I read each paper

  1. Abstract and conclusion first, to learn what it claims.
  2. The parameter and performance tables, since that is where "practical" gets tested.
  3. Only then the construction, and the proofs only if I need them.
  4. One sentence in my notes: what problem did this solve that the previous paper couldn't?
Takeaway: the field is a sequence of removing bottlenecks, namely noise, bootstrapping cost and non-linearity. Each paper is the answer to the previous paper's biggest limitation.
